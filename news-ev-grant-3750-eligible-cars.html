Which cars qualify for the £3,750 Electric Car Grant?

Purchase support • Updated December 2025

Quick summary

  • The Electric Car Grant (ECG) offers up to £3,750 off a new electric car that meets certain criteria.
  • The scheme has been extended to around 2030 with extra government funding.
  • There are effectively two main grant levels for cars: £3,750 (top band) and £1,500 (lower band).
  • The discount is applied automatically by the dealer – you don’t have to claim it separately.

Basic rules – who and what is eligible?

The grant is aimed at making new, fully electric cars more affordable for private buyers and small businesses. In simple terms:

  • The car must be fully electric (battery-electric), not a plug-in hybrid.
  • There is a price cap – only cars with a list price below a set threshold (around the mid-£30k range) qualify.
  • The manufacturer has to be signed up to the scheme and meet certain sustainability criteria.

If all of that is true, the grant is knocked off the price at the point of sale. It doesn’t affect any finance incentives or dealer discounts you may also be offered.

The two grant levels

Official guidance and industry coverage describe two main grant levels for cars:

  • Band One – £3,750 discount on selected models that meet stricter efficiency and sustainability criteria.
  • Band Two – £1,500 discount on a wider range of eligible electric cars.

Examples of cars that can qualify for the £3,750 grant

The exact list changes as new models are launched or reclassified, but as of early December 2025, examples of models reported as qualifying for the full £3,750 include:

  • Ford Puma Gen-E (compact crossover)
  • Nissan Leaf (family hatchback)
  • Citroën ë-C5 Aircross Long Range
  • Ford E-Tourneo Courier
  • Renault 5 E-Tech (52 kWh version)
  • Renault 4 E-Tech
  • Alpine A290
  • Mini Countryman Electric

Manufacturers such as Renault and MINI also highlight that several of their E-Tech / electric models qualify for either the £1,500 or £3,750 bands depending on specification.

Examples of cars that can qualify for the £1,500 grant

The lower band still represents a useful saving on a new EV. Examples regularly mentioned in industry coverage include models like the Citroën ë-C3 and other compact EVs positioned as affordable city or family cars.

Because pricing and trim levels change regularly, dealers will usually confirm exactly which versions of a car are eligible when you configure or order it.

How do I know if a specific car qualifies?

In practice, there are three simple checks:

  1. Ask the dealer directly. They should be able to confirm whether a particular trim and battery size is currently on the approved list and how much discount is applied.
  2. Check the list price. If the car you’re looking at is well above the scheme’s price cap, it’s unlikely to qualify.
  3. Watch out for “on-the-road” quotes. Often, the grant has already been included in the advertised price, so the saving is baked in rather than shown as a separate line.

What does the grant mean for used EV buyers?

Even if you’re only interested in used EVs, the grant still matters. Today’s subsidised new cars become tomorrow’s used stock, and stronger upfront support can:

  • Increase the number of EVs entering the used market over the next few years.
  • Help keep residual values more stable, because demand is spread across more price points.
  • Make popular grant-eligible models like the Leaf, Puma Gen-E and Renault 5 more common on forecourts and classified sites in future.

When those cars do appear as used EVs, battery health will be one of the biggest factors in deciding whether they represent good value. Two cars can look identical on paper but have noticeably different State of Health percentages, depending on how they’ve been used and charged.
